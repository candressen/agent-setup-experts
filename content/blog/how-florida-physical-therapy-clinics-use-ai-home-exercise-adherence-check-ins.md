# How Florida Physical Therapy Clinics Use AI for Home Exercise Adherence Check-Ins

A physical therapist can build an excellent home exercise program, demonstrate every movement, and send clear instructions. The difficult part begins after the patient leaves the clinic.

Did the patient complete the exercises? Was the schedule realistic? Did pain increase? Was one movement confusing? Did work, travel, transportation, or caregiving responsibilities interrupt the plan?

When the next appointment is several days away, those questions often remain unanswered. The therapist may not discover the problem until the patient returns with limited progress or admits that the program stopped after the first day.

That is why **Florida physical therapy clinics are using AI for home exercise adherence check-ins**. A focused workflow can send brief, approved messages between visits, collect structured replies, identify common barriers, and route clinical concerns to the appropriate team member.

## Why Home Exercise Programs Lose Momentum

Most patients do not abandon a home program because they are careless. The breakdown usually happens for practical reasons.

A patient may forget which exercise comes first. The printed sheet may be in the car. A video link may be difficult to find. The prescribed frequency may conflict with a long shift. Mild soreness may create uncertainty about whether to continue. Another patient may feel no immediate improvement and quietly decide the program is not working.

Clinic teams know these barriers exist, but manual follow-up is difficult to deliver consistently. Therapists move from evaluation to treatment to documentation. Front-desk staff are not positioned to answer clinical questions. Calling every patient between visits is rarely realistic.

The result is a visibility gap: the clinic has a plan, but no reliable signal about what is happening at home.

## What an AI Adherence Check-In Can Do

A useful check-in is short and specific. It does not ask the patient to write a long progress report. Instead, it gathers a few practical signals that help the clinic decide what should happen next.

For example, the workflow might ask whether the patient completed the assigned routine, which exercise was hardest to perform, and whether symptoms were better, unchanged, or worse afterward. The response can be written back to the approved patient record or task system.

The workflow can then sort replies into categories such as:

- completed as planned;
- missed because of schedule or forgetfulness;
- needs instructions resent;
- reports difficulty with a specific movement;
- reports worsening symptoms or another clinical concern;
- did not respond after the approved follow-up sequence.

This structure gives staff a usable queue instead of a collection of scattered texts and voicemails.

## A Practical Check-In Workflow

A Florida physical therapy clinic can start with one simple sequence tied to a clearly defined patient group.

### 1. Trigger the Message

The first check-in might be sent one or two days after an evaluation, after a home program changes, or midway between visits. Timing should match the clinic's care process rather than sending the same message to everyone.

### 2. Ask Approved Questions

The message should use clinic-approved language and avoid making a diagnosis. A straightforward prompt could ask whether the patient completed the program and whether anything prevented completion.

### 3. Classify the Reply

AI can recognize common nonclinical barriers, such as forgetting, losing the instructions, or not having enough time. It can also identify replies that require human review.

### 4. Provide an Approved Next Step

If the patient needs the existing instructions or video link again, the workflow can resend the approved resource. It can also offer a simple reminder option when permitted by clinic policy.

### 5. Escalate Clinical Concerns

Reports of increased pain, new symptoms, falls, swelling, dizziness, or uncertainty about safe performance should go to a therapist or designated clinical queue. The system should not improvise exercise changes.

### 6. Record the Outcome

The clinic should be able to see whether the patient responded, what category was assigned, what resource was sent, and whether the case was escalated.

## Where Human Review Still Matters

AI can improve consistency, but it should remain inside a narrow operational role.

A therapist still decides whether an exercise is appropriate, whether a program should change, and how symptoms affect the plan of care. Staff should review ambiguous responses, clinical questions, and any message that falls outside the approved workflow.

The clinic also needs clear rules for consent, message channels, documentation, access controls, retention, and protected health information. A healthcare workflow should be designed around the clinic's privacy and compliance requirements from the beginning.

## What Clinics Can Measure

The goal is not simply to send more messages. The goal is to reduce the time between a patient encountering a barrier and the clinic recognizing it.

Useful measures include response rate, percentage of patients reporting completion, number of instruction-resend requests, clinical escalations, unresolved nonresponses, and adherence trends before the next appointment.

Clinics can also compare attendance and progress indicators for patients who receive check-ins with similar patients who do not. The data will not explain every outcome, but it can reveal whether the workflow is creating earlier visibility.

## Start With One Patient Group

The safest rollout is usually narrow. A clinic might begin with patients who have uncomplicated orthopedic plans, a predictable visit cadence, and an established set of approved resources.

Define the exact trigger, questions, categories, escalation rules, and documentation destination. Review the first set of conversations manually. Adjust the wording when patients misunderstand a question or when too many replies land in the general review queue.

Once the clinic trusts the process, it can consider other use cases such as appointment preparation, post-discharge check-ins, or long-term maintenance reminders.

## Build a Follow-Up System Patients Can Actually Use

Home exercise adherence is not solved by one reminder. It improves when patients can report a barrier quickly and the clinic can respond through a controlled, visible process.
